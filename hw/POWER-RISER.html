The per-column unit

Block and board live in one box, one above the other. A single pair enters from the supply. Twelve fused pairs, twelve data leads and one Cat5e come out. Four of these make a display.

  1. The three conductors converge at the supply, not at the unitThe supply’s three +V screws each carry a third of the load, because one M5 screw cannot carry sixty amps. Short 12 AWG pigtails leave those screws and are crimped into one 6 AWG lug within a foot. 3 × 12 AWG is 9.9 mm² into a 13.3 mm² barrel, a 75 % fill. From there a single 6 AWG pair runs 4.5 ft to the unit and lands as one ring per stud.
  2. The fuses stay upstream of the convergenceEach 12 AWG pigtail keeps its own 30 A inline fuse before the three meet. Merging the conductors does not merge their protection. 30 A, not 25 A: each conductor carries about 20 A at the cap, blade fuses derate for continuous duty, and these are in parallel — blow one at 25 A and the other two jump to 30 A each and follow it, taking the column with them. At 30 A the working current is 67 % of rating and that cascade does not start. Make the pigtails 12 to 18 in: an ATC inline holder is three inches long, and six-inch pigtails come out more holder than wire.
  3. The controller is the thirteenth circuitThe block has twelve positions and the column has twelve panels, so the board’s 5 V tap has nowhere to sit. It comes off the + stud through its own 5 A inline holder. Its return doubles onto a negative screw beside a panel return — not onto the negative stud, which is already at ABYC’s four-terminal limit with the three supply conductors on it.
  4. Only the 5 V tap crosses between the halvesPanel current never touches the board. The board’s 0 V is the column’s signal reference and nothing else, which is why it is a tap off the same bus rather than a pass-through.
  5. The convergence is a connector, not a spliceOne Anderson SB120-06-GRY carries both poles, so a single mated pair per column detaches the whole unit for bench work. Genderless, colour-keyed so gray only mates gray, polarised so + cannot reach −, and 120 A rated against a 60 A cap. It costs 0.5 mΩ per mated pole — 0.06 V across the two — which is the price of a joint you can open. SB50 would not do: it is rated 50 A.

Mounting: four units, two doors

The four units for a display sit on two hinged doors in a 33 in bay on the side of the frame, two units to a door, hinged at the outer edges so both swing out from the centre. Everything that needs a meter or a fuse puller is reachable without going near the panels.

A hinged door is a moving cable bundle

Twelve drops, twelve data leads, one Cat5e and one supply feed all cross the hinge line on every unit. Give each bundle a service loop sized for the door at full swing, dress it along the hinge axis rather than across it, and clamp it on both sides of the hinge so the flex happens in the loop and not at a terminal.

The feed is the one to watch: it is the stiffest bundle in the build and the only one carrying fifty amps. If the door will not open with the feed connected, the feed is too short — do not fix it by opening the door less.

Still open

  • Bay height above the floor. Mid-column is what halves the drop lengths, and the door band sets it for all four columns at once. Fix this before cutting any 14 AWG.
  • Which side of the frame. Whichever it is, the data loom has to cross the wall once at the unit to reach the far stile. Decide the side and the crossing together.
  • Enclosure depth. Measure the block with its cover latched, plus the ring terminal stack on the studs, plus the devkit in its socket, before the box is made.
  • Whether the supplied device terminals take 14 AWG. The block ships 24 of them for the twelve circuits, but kits like this usually size them 16–22 AWG. If they will not take the 14 AWG drops, buy rings that will — and rings rather than forks, because a fork walks out from under a screw that backs off and a ring cannot.
  • The block’s bus material. Amazon’s spec table says “Stainless Steel”, which is almost certainly garbage referring to the screws — but stainless has roughly forty times copper’s resistivity and this bus carries 60 A. Scratch the plating and look for copper, or measure across the bus and expect milliohms.
  • Stud thread and usable length. Reported as M5, #4–6 AWG, but not published anywhere on the listing. The two supplied power terminals answer it on arrival: their hole size is the stud size.
  • The blown-fuse LEDs at 5 V. They are sized for 12 V and will pass about 3 mA here instead of 10 mA. Check whether they are actually visible in room light before relying on them to find a blown circuit.

One column, floor to top

Where the cable goes, not where the boxes sit. The block half and the PCB half are drawn apart here for legibility — physically they share the one enclosure of E-1. Twelve light pairs leave it, six up and six down, and peel out of the loom at the row they serve.

  1. The loom thins as it climbsFive pairs leave the block in each direction. One pair peels off at every row, so the bundle that reaches row 1 carries a single drop. Size the split loom for the thick end.
  2. Unit at mid height, not at the floorNo drop is then longer than half the wall. The 2 m leads already on order reach every row with slack, and the worst-case voltage drop halves. The door band sets this height for all four columns at once.
  3. Split the two looms at the door, deliberatelyBoth now leave the same enclosure, so geometry no longer separates them for you. Power climbs one stile; data crosses at the unit and climbs the other. Twelve 5 V drops and twelve data leads in one loom is the mistake that is expensive to undo later.
  4. Screw-mount P-clips, every 12 to 18 inNot adhesive. 14 AWG silicone is heavy, the air at the top of the wall is warm, and adhesive clips let go. Take the weight of both looms at the top, not at the connectors.
  5. A fuse on every feeder conductorThree 12 AWG in parallel need three fuses. One protected conductor does nothing for its neighbours if one of them chafes through. 30 A inline at the supply, where the energy is.
  6. Anderson PP30 at every panelCrimp, genderless, keyed by housing colour. A dead panel comes out in ten seconds with no soldering iron at the top of a ladder. That is the whole point of the connector.

The load, honestly

A 16×16 panel at full white is 256 LEDs at 60 mA, so 15.4 A. Twelve of them is 184 A a column and 737 A a display. Nobody builds 737 A of 5 V distribution, and nothing you would ever show needs it.

So the firmware caps each column at 60 A and scales the whole frame down to fit. That is a third of full white, which for real content is generous: a frame has to be almost entirely white before the cap even engages. The supply is sized to the cap, not to the arithmetic.

This sheet depends on one line of firmware

The 60 A budget is enforced in software, not in hardware. FastLED.setMaxPowerInVoltsAndMilliamps(5, 60000) is in the bench firmware. It is not yet in the production handoff. Without it, a white frame asks a 60 A supply for 184 A, and what happens next depends on which supply you bought. Add it before a column is ever fed from its own supply.

One display, one line

Four identical circuits, mains to pixels, with the data path underneath. The only thing crossing between columns is Ethernet.

Size the circuit on RMS current, not on watts

Four columns at their cap is 1 437 W of real power, which looks like 12 A at 120 V. That is only true if the supply has power factor correction. The LRS-350 does not. Its datasheet has no power-factor row at all, and Note 10 states it fails EN61000-3-2, the harmonic current standard. Its PF is about 0.5, so it draws roughly twice the RMS current its wattage implies — and a breaker trips on RMS.

LRS-350-5: about 24 A RMS for four. That needs two 20 A circuits per display, four for both walls.
RSP-320-5: PF > 0.98, about 12 A RMS for four. One 20 A circuit per display, with margin.

The RSP is about $55 more each. Eight of them costs $440 and removes two dedicated circuits from the build, which is cheaper than an electrician. Its inrush is 20 A against the LRS’s 60 A, so four of them cold-starting together will not trip a healthy breaker either. Neither supply has remote sense; you trim the ADJ pot by hand on both.

One drop, end to end

Ninety-six of these get built. Every choice below is about doing it ninety-six times without hating it, and about getting one panel out again afterwards.

Why 20 A on a 15.4 A load

A blade fuse carries its rating continuously and opens at roughly twice it. A 15 A fuse would sit at 103 % whenever a panel went white, and it would nuisance-blow at the worst possible moment. 20 A leaves the panel its full output and still protects 14 AWG silicone, which is good for about 32 A in free air.

Why not copper-clad aluminium

Cheap “silicone” wire on marketplaces is often CCA. It has around 60 % of the conductivity for the same gauge, it work-hardens, and it creeps in a crimp until the joint heats up. At 15 A a foot from a panel that is not a saving. Buy stranded pure copper and check the strand count on arrival.

Two contact sizes, one housing

A PP30 contact’s barrel is cut for 12–14 AWG. The panels ship with thinner power leads, usually 18 or 20 AWG, and 18 AWG rattles inside a PP30 barrel and will not crimp. Use a PP15 contact on that side. The 1327 housing is identical across PP15, PP30 and PP45, so a PP15 contact mates a PP30 contact with no adapter and no second connector system.

That makes the joint 15 A against a panel’s 15.4 A absolute peak, and it changes nothing real. The panel’s own 18 AWG lead is already the narrowest conductor in the chain and the manufacturer chose it. A fatter contact would not fix the wire behind it.

Check one panel before ordering contacts

Measure the gauge of its power leads, and look for screw terminals on the back. If the panels have terminals, take that route instead: a short 14 AWG panel tail, ferrules at the panel and a PP30 pair at the other end. The tail stays with the panel, the whole joint is 30 A, and it still unplugs in seconds.

Mechanical

  • Take the weight at the top. Both looms hang from the top rail. The P-clips below guide the run; they do not carry it. No connector should ever be in tension.
  • Six inches of slack at every panel. One full turn of service loop before the connector. It is what lets you unclip a panel and turn it over on the ladder.
  • Split loom, two sizes. 3/8 in for the thick end of the bundle, 1/4 in past row 3 where it is down to a pair or two.
  • Power up one stile, data up the other. Cheap insurance against pickup on a 400 ns data edge, and it makes the wall readable when something is wrong.
  • Label both ends of every drop with the column and row, C2-R07. Do it as you build. Nobody has ever traced 96 unlabelled red wires and enjoyed it.

Commissioning, in this order

  1. Bench the supply alone. Set ADJ to 5.00 V with nothing connected. Do this before it is four feet up a wall.
  2. Check + against − with the supply unplugged. It will beep for a moment and stop. That is normal: a discharged output capacitor is indistinguishable from a short until the meter’s own 1 mA has charged it, which takes about a tenth of a second. Then the reading climbs into the kΩ and beyond. A beep that never stops is the fault. Short the terminals before you repeat the test, or the caps are still charged and you will see nothing.
  3. Fit the three feeder pairs, one conductor per screw. Never bridge two supply terminals with one wire. Confirm polarity at the block with the supply off, then power up with no drops fitted.
  4. Fuse one drop, connect one panel, run the bench firmware. Then add drops one row at a time. A wiring error costs one panel, not twelve.
  5. Drive the column to full white with the 60 A cap in force. Measure at the row 1 and row 12 terminals, not at the supply.
  6. Trim ADJ until the furthest panel reads 5.00 V under load. Expect about 5.15 V at the supply. Never exceed 5.3 V at the nearest panel.
  7. Come back after ten minutes with an IR thermometer. Block terminals, feeder lugs and every Powerpole should be near ambient. Anything above about 50 °C is a bad crimp, and it will only get worse.

Revisions to what I told you before

Anderson PP30 replaces XT30. XT30’s solder cup does not take 14 AWG, its ready-made pigtails come with 16 AWG, and 96 panels is 384 solder joints either way. Powerpole is crimp-only, genderless, and the same housing family the rest of the DC world uses. It costs about $60 more per display and one $45 crimp tool.

RSP-320-5 replaces LRS-350-5, and 3 × 12 AWG replaces the 6 AWG feeder. The LRS has no power factor correction, so four of them pull about 24 A RMS rather than the 12 A their wattage suggests. And 6 AWG will not fit the supply’s M4 terminal anyway: the three +V screws are parallel taps on one rail, meant to be fed by three conductors. Running those three the whole way up deletes the bus bar, the 6 AWG and the lug crimper for the same money and 0.06 V.

The block and the controller are now one unit on a hinged door. They were drawn facing each other across the frame, on the theory that power up one stile and data up the other falls out of the geometry. It does not any more: both looms leave the same box, so the split has to be made deliberately at the door. What the change buys is access — every fuse, every terminal and the devkit’s USB port are reachable by opening a door, without a ladder and without touching a panel.

The three feeder conductors now converge at the supply, not at the unit. They were going to run the whole 4.5 ft as three pairs and meet on the block’s stud. Instead, short fused 12 AWG pigtails leave the supply’s six screws and are crimped into one 6 AWG lug per polarity within a foot, and a single 6 AWG welding-cable pair makes the run. The electrical case is a wash — 13.3 mm² of copper against 9.9 mm², 0.21 V against 0.25 V. The real reason is the door: conductors crossing the hinge drop from six to two, and one flexible cable dresses around a hinge in a way a six-conductor bundle never will. Welding cable, not building wire, for the same reason.

A 6 AWG ring on the stud is still how it lands, so the ABYC four-per-stud question is moot either way. The individual 30 A fuses stay in the pigtails, upstream of the convergence: merging the conductors does not merge their protection.

The distribution block is a DaierTek 12-way, $15.99 (Amazon B0CGT91KTR). Twelve circuits, 100 A per block, 30 A per circuit, 32 V DC, transparent cover with a push latch, blown-fuse LEDs. It ships with two power terminals alongside twenty-four device terminals, which is what settles the thing that mattered: dedicated + and − inputs, separate from the twelve output pairs, so all twelve negative positions stay free for panel returns. Its stud is M5, rated #4–6 AWG.

The Blue Sea 5026 was specified here first at $43.29, and publishes everything the DaierTek does not — #10-32 studs both sides, tinned copper buses. That is the trade: $110 a display against two specifications that now have to be checked on arrival instead of before ordering. Both are in Still open.

The supply on hand is an S-350-5, 5 V 70 A. Derated to about 50 A continuous for an unbranded unit, that is one full column with the firmware cap set to match, or roughly a quarter of full white across twelve panels. It has no power factor correction, so the RMS argument below still points at the RSP-320-5 for the finished wall — one of these is fine on a 15 A circuit, four are not.

The convergence is an SB120 connector, decided. A sealed butt splice was the cheaper option and is now rejected: across eight columns, being able to unplug a unit and carry it to the bench is worth more than the $283 and the 0.06 V. One housing carries both poles, so it is two kits a column, not four. The 0.06 V is the honest cost — it puts the feeder at 0.273 V, over the 0.25 V target, recoverable on the supply’s ADJ because every panel sits downstream of it.

The bill is about $750 a display, not $520. I under-counted the 14 AWG: 48 drops averaging a metre is 320 ft of wire once you count both conductors, and that alone is $144. The rest of the design is unchanged.
